Figure 2 — The engine's edge survives estimated CPTs

Error vs k under CPTs MLE-estimated from finite data (n=200). Engine error = the irreducible estimation floor; the LLM adds reasoning error that emerges at k≥4.
Figure 2. Both pay the same irreducible estimation floor (the best any method can do with that data; it shrinks with n and does not explode with k). At low k the LLM ties it; at k≥4 the LLM's error rises above the floor as its in-head marginalization degrades. The engine adds no error of its own. (The k=6 dip is the anchoring artifact of the uniform generator, §5.3 — see the de-anchored Figure 1 for the clean collapse.)

Figure 3 — Financial case study: credit-risk causal attribution

Left: the cliff in credit-default attribution. Right: the 2008 copula failure mode — normal-times calibration looks fine on average, catastrophic in the tail.
(a) P(default | do(distress)) accuracy vs systemic factors
(b) CPT miscalibration bias (normal-times vs crisis)
Figure 3. (a) gpt-5.1's credit-default causal attribution collapses (0.32→0.04→0.00) as systemic factors grow; the engine is exact. (b) A CPT calibrated on normal times underestimates the *unconditional* do-effect by only 0.05 (crisis configs are rare and down-weighted), but the **stressed** measure — what risk management acts on — by 0.23–0.28. The engine computes both exactly; its credential certifies the computation, not that the CPT reflects crisis conditions (the 2008 copula failure mode).

Figure 4. deepseek spent 1.50M tokens (79% on wrong answers); gpt-5.1 spent 0.86M (63% on wrong). At high complexity (k≥5) deepseek spent 917k tokens to get 0/100 correct. The engine spends ~0 tokens and is exact. You pay the most to be confidently misled exactly where the problem is hardest.
